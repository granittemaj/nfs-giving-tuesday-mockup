How to organize Giving Tuesday at work

The short answer

Giving Tuesday at work can be a company donation, an employee-led activity or an offer of useful expertise. The first decision is who is making the commitment. A business giving its own funds, colleagues donating personally and staff contributing working time are different arrangements, even when they support the same cause.

Agree that distinction before sending an invitation. It determines who approves the budget, who manages the activity and what can be said publicly. A small, well-explained contribution is easier for colleagues to join than a campaign whose purpose or expectations remain unclear.

Choose the right participation model

ModelWhat it involvesWhat needs confirming
Company giftThe business donates its own moneyBudget, recipient, purpose and approval
Employee givingIndividuals choose whether to donateVoluntary participation and official giving route
Employer matchThe company adds money under agreed conditionsEligibility, limits, timing and administration
Staff time or expertisePeople carry out an agreed taskRecipient need, scope, paid time and supervision

These models can be combined, but explain them separately. If the company gives $1,000 and employees donate privately, report the company gift without guessing the employee total. If staff spend time on a task, describe the work rather than assigning it an invented financial value.

Choose a cause the team can understand

Explain why the cause matters and what the recipient does. The purpose should be specific enough for a colleague to describe without repeating a slogan. If the team is considering Not For Sale, begin with the case for support and choose one issue guide as background reading.

Human trafficking and environmental harm can raise questions about work, recruitment, purchasing and the use of natural resources. A workplace discussion can examine those connections without pretending that every business has the same risks. Do not imply that making a donation certifies the company’s labor practices or environmental performance.

Make participation genuinely voluntary

Offer colleagues a way to learn or contribute time without making a personal donation. Avoid public giving leaderboards, requests to disclose why someone declined or pressure from line managers. People’s finances and relationships with particular causes are private.

Consider practical access as well. Can remote staff take part? Does an activity fall outside someone’s working hours? Is a venue accessible? Can a colleague join without appearing in photographs? These questions are part of organizing an inclusive activity, not additions to address after the invitation has gone out.

Give each task an owner

The organizer should know who approves company spending, verifies the donation route and answers questions. If a payment requires a supplier record, tax information or another internal process, speak to finance early. Do not promise a transfer date that depends on approvals nobody has requested.

For an event or volunteer task, identify the contact at the recipient organization and agree how much coordination it can provide. A charity may not have capacity to create a special activity for a large team. Ask about existing needs before planning the company’s preferred experience.

PeriodMain workWhat should be settled

Final weekCheck the detailsLinks, timing, access and question handling

This is a suggested planning schedule, not a rule about when participation must start. If time is short, choose a simpler activity. A company donation with a clear explanation may be more practical than organizing an event and expecting a charity to accommodate it at short notice.

How to handle employer matching

Start by asking whether a program already exists. If it does, use its actual eligibility rules and submission process. If it does not, a new matching offer needs approval before it appears in employee communications.

A usable offer states who can participate, which organizations qualify, the matching rate, any per-person or total cap, the dates covered and how gifts will be confirmed. It should also explain what happens if requests exceed the budget. The employee should not have to discover the most important restriction after making a donation.

Sample inquiry: “We are considering a Giving Tuesday activity supporting [charity]. Could you confirm whether employee gifts qualify for matching, the limits and deadline, and how receipts should be submitted? Please also let us know how the company confirms that the match has been paid.”

A team session that leads somewhere

For a short learning session, choose one question rather than trying to explain the entire mission. A useful topic might be how recruitment deception becomes exploitation, or what a tree-planting figure can and cannot show. Ask people to read a short guide in advance and discuss what they found clear or unresolved.

End with optional routes: read more, ask a question, make a gift or offer a skill. Keep the donation link available without making payment the price of participation. Do not ask colleagues to share experiences of abuse or exploitation as part of an unplanned workplace discussion.

An illustrative plan for a small business

A small business agrees to make a company donation and hold a twenty-minute discussion on Giving Tuesday. Finance confirms the recipient and payment process. The organizer shares the mission page a week ahead and gives colleagues a private link if they want to donate personally. Participation in personal giving is optional.

Afterward, the company reports the amount it actually paid and the fact that the discussion took place. It does not claim that every employee supported the cause or that a particular number of people will be protected because of the gift. A colleague who wishes to offer specialist help contacts the charity separately to discuss a defined task.

This example is deliberately modest. It gives the company, employees and recipient clear roles, and it avoids creating a reporting burden larger than the contribution.

Supporting Not For Sale as a business

Start with the mission and decide whether you are making a donation or proposing a longer relationship. If you want to explore a partnership or offer expertise, contact Not For Sale with a specific outline: what your organization does, what it can contribute and who would be responsible.

For straightforward giving, use the Giving Tuesday donation page. Describe your support accurately in any public communication. A donation can contribute to the mission without giving the donor authority to speak for the organization.

Giving Tuesday questions
Can employees choose their own causes?+

That depends on the activity or company program. If a company has selected one recipient, explain that choice. If employees can nominate charities, publish the decision process before asking for suggestions.

Should we collect money ourselves?+

Sharing the charity’s official donation route is simpler for a basic activity. If the business collects funds, finance should establish the handling, records and transfer arrangements first.

Can remote colleagues participate?+

Plan for that from the start. Provide the same source material and giving information, and choose a discussion format or alternative action they can access.

Can we require everyone to donate?+

The approach in this guide is voluntary personal giving. A company can decide on its own corporate donation without treating employees’ private money as part of that commitment.

Can volunteering happen during paid time?+

Only where the employer agrees. Confirm the working-time arrangement, responsibilities and recipient’s needs before offering the activity.

What should we publish afterward?+

Confirmed company gifts, completed activities and any other results you can support. Keep employee names and donation amounts private unless there is a clear, appropriate agreement to share them.

Can we use the Gift of Giving for staff?+

The current page explains a proposed experience. It is not an operational employee-gifting service. Do not promise delivery to colleagues on the basis of the concept alone.

What if the charity cannot host us?+

Ask whether another contribution would help, or choose an existing opportunity elsewhere. The recipient’s capacity should shape the offer.
