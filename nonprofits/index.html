How to plan a Giving Tuesday campaign for your charity

The short answer

A Giving Tuesday campaign asks people to make a decision. They need to understand the problem, the organization’s response and what their contribution will support. The campaign team needs an equally clear account of the purpose, the evidence it can publish and the practical work required to receive and acknowledge support.

For December 1, 2026, begin with those decisions before choosing a slogan or commissioning campaign graphics. An attractive appeal cannot resolve uncertainty about the funding purpose. The plan below is an editorial and operational framework you can use on this page, adapted to the people and resources available to your organization. 01

Agree the funding purpose

Write a short internal statement: “This campaign seeks support for [purpose], because [need]. Donations will be used for [general mission or agreed restriction]. The person responsible for approving this account is [role].” If different members of the team would fill those fields differently, resolve the disagreement before launching.

A mission-wide appeal can be specific. Explain the activities, responsibilities and needs that the organization must sustain. You do not have to manufacture a single project to make the invitation concrete. Equally, if the appeal is restricted, the public wording should reflect the actual terms and how the organization will handle funds under those terms.

Choose an audience you can reach

Separate existing supporters from people encountering the charity for the first time. A long-standing donor may need an update and a clear giving route. A new reader may first need an explanation of the organization and evidence of its work. The same page can serve both if it provides a direct action near the top and a coherent account below.

Identify how you will reach each group through channels you already have the capacity to use. A campaign does not become stronger simply by appearing on every platform. An unanswered question on a new channel can create more work than the extra reach justifies.

Build the case from evidence

Choose the facts and accounts that explain the work, then confirm their sources and permissions. Keep the reporting period attached to a number. Distinguish an activity from an outcome and an example from a typical result. If you cannot substantiate a donation-to-outcome calculation, explain the work without assigning a fictional unit price.

A person’s experience should not be edited into a simpler story merely because a campaign needs a clear ending. Seek appropriate consent, protect privacy and respect decisions about what can be shared. Where a real account cannot be used responsibly, describe the program and the evidence rather than inventing a composite survivor.

Make the appeal answer the reader’s questions

Reader questionWhat the page should provide
What is happeningA clear description of the need
What do you do about itA concrete explanation of the response
Why should I believe thisRelevant sources, reporting and attribution
What will my money supportAn accurate funding purpose
How do I giveA clear action and understandable payment choices
What happens afterwardConfirmation and a reliable help route

Arrange the page in that order where it helps the reader. Avoid delaying the explanation behind a long emotional introduction. A direct answer can still have depth; the supporting sections should earn the reader’s attention by explaining what the opening cannot cover alone.

This schedule is a recommendation, not an official GivingTuesday timetable. If you start later, reduce the number of activities. Preserve the time needed to check the donation experience and ensure that the appeal says what the organization intends.

Write a small set of coordinated messages

Prepare one central explanation and adapt it to the channel. An email can explain the need and response, while a short social post can introduce one point and link to the page. A partner message may need to explain why that organization is involved. The funding purpose should remain consistent across them.

Do not confuse variety with changing the promise. If one message asks for general support and another suggests every gift is reserved for one activity, supporters may reasonably understand the campaign in different ways. Review the messages together before publication.

A basic appeal structure is: a clear need; the organization’s response; evidence the reader can inspect; the funding purpose; one next action. The toolkit contains complete starter messages, including a results update and thank-you.

Check the giving experience

Follow the route from the appeal to the donation service on a phone and a desktop. Check the amount, currency and frequency choices. Read any optional fee prompt. Confirm who receives the money and whether the chosen purpose carries through from the appeal. Review the acknowledgment and support arrangements with the person responsible for the service.

Use an approved test method for transactions. A page that opens successfully does not establish that payment, receipt delivery and recurring-donation management all work. Record what was tested and what remains dependent on an external provider.

Also test reading and navigation. Can a person use the page with a keyboard? Does enlarged text remain readable? Is a table usable on a small screen? Does every button say what it will do? These checks serve donors directly and should not be deferred because the page looks attractive at one desktop size.

Set matching terms before promoting them

If a funder offers matching money, document the arrangement and translate it accurately into public language. State the qualifying period, eligible gifts, rate and cap. Clarify whether the commitment has conditions beyond donations received, and how the result will be reported.

When the cap is reached, update the message. Do not leave an unlimited “double your gift” promise on the page after the available match has been exhausted. A future pledge from a funder should remain distinct from money received in the charity’s records and public update.

Decide what success means

Choose measures that fit the campaign. A donation appeal may track completed gifts and received funds. A volunteer appeal may track eligible inquiries, agreed placements and completed contributions. A learning event may track attendance and the questions it helped resolve. Avoid choosing a metric only because it produces a large number.

Illustrative calculation: if a campaign receives 2,000 relevant visits and records 40 completed donations from those visits, its measured visit-to-donation rate is 2 percent. That calculation depends on consistent tracking and definitions; it is not a benchmark or a forecast for Not For Sale. If cross-domain tracking loses the link between visits and payments, report that limitation instead of presenting a precise but unreliable rate.

Report the result people can trust

Reconcile funds and distinguish received gifts, pledges and expected matches. State the period and whether the total is gross or after specified costs. If you report volunteer work, describe completed tasks rather than combining unlike contributions into an unexplained score.

Thank people before turning the message into another appeal. If the work funded continues beyond Giving Tuesday, explain when a meaningful update will be possible. Do not promise immediate evidence of long-term change simply because the fundraising period has ended.

What this hub contributes

Not For Sale’s approach is to make Giving Tuesday useful to people who are still deciding how to participate, while explaining our own mission clearly. The educational guides should be worth reading even when the visitor does not donate. The case for support then gives interested readers a direct route to examine the organization and make a gift.

That is a strategy to assess, not a guarantee of traffic or fundraising performance. Each organization should choose its campaign according to its audience, capacity and work.

Giving Tuesday questions
Do we need a large target?+

Use a target when it has a credible basis in needs and fundraising capacity. Explain what it represents and how you will report progress.

Is a longer appeal always better?+

No. Include enough detail to answer the decision the reader faces. Use clear sections so someone ready to give can act and someone needing context can examine it.

Should resources require an email address?+

This hub keeps practical content open on the page. If your organization requests contact details elsewhere, explain the purpose and treat marketing preferences clearly.

Can we use artificial intelligence to draft copy?+

Drafting support does not remove responsibility for accuracy, permissions or voice. Check claims against their sources and rewrite text that misrepresents the organization or the people involved.

What if we do not have a match?+

Make the case for the work without one. Do not imply that Giving Tuesday automatically adds money to a donation.

What should happen if the payment route fails?+

Provide a clear contact route, correct the fault and update public messages. Do not continue directing people into a known broken experience without explanation.

When should we publish results?+

When the relevant figures have been reconciled. An initial thank-you can precede the final total if you explain what remains pending.

What should we keep for next year?+

The approved purpose, source record, final messages, measured results and notes on what caused confusion. These provide a useful basis for the next campaign without requiring the team to repeat every decision.
